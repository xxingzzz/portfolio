StepUp

Most people know prolonged sitting is harmful—yet they still struggle to move. StepUp explores why awareness doesn't lead to action, and how small, everyday movements can be designed into habits.

Role:

Product Designer

Timeline:

Jan-Feb 2023

Context

Prolonged sitting is a silent but normalized health risk among students and young professionals

illustration — sedentary lifestyle scene
💬Sitting all day became his normal — This project started from someone very close to me—my boyfriend. During his student years, he spent long hours sitting due to academic pressure and job hunting. Over time, he began experiencing persistent discomfort in his neck and lower back. At first, it felt like a common consequence of studying hard—almost something to tolerate.
🔍A personal problem, a universal risk — But as I looked deeper, I realized this was part of a much larger issue. Prolonged sitting is not only linked to temporary discomfort, but also to long-term health risks such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and spinal issues.

The "solution" seemed obvious at first: just exercise more. But reality didn't match this assumption. Even when he understood the importance of movement, he struggled to start and even more to sustain it. That contradiction stayed with me.

Question

If people already know sitting is harmful, why is it still so hard to change behavior?

I began noticing similar patterns around me. Many students and peers: sit for long hours in classrooms or at desks, remain sedentary during breaks (scrolling, gaming, watching videos), and experience physical discomfort—some occasional, some persistent. What stood out was not ignorance—but awareness without action. This challenged my initial belief that education alone could solve the problem.

Why do people fail to act even when they already know what is good for them?

This question became the starting point of my exploration.

Exploration

Initial assumptions pointed to time, motivation, and resources—but reality was more complex

Like many people, I first turned to online research. The most common explanations were:

⏳lack of time
🔥lack of motivation
💰lack of financial access

At first glance, these felt reasonable. I even tried to reason through them myself: If people had more time, would they move more? If motivation improved, would behavior change last? If access to gyms or tools increased, would consistency follow?

But something about these answers felt incomplete. They explained barriers on the surface—but not why people still failed even when those barriers were reduced.

illustration — assumptions vs. reality

So I decided to test these assumptions directly. I conducted six one-on-one interviews with people who identified as having sedentary lifestyles. Instead of focusing on what they knew, I focused on what they actually did—and more importantly, what they failed to do despite intending to act.

I wasn't just listening for answers. I was looking for contradictions. And I found them.

Insight

The real barrier is not external constraints, but internalized habits and behavioral defaults

This is where my understanding began to shift.

⏰

Insight 1 — Time is not the real constraint; habit is the default driver of behavior

At first glance, time appeared to be the limiting factor. But interviews showed a different pattern: People who are naturally active still move even when busy, people who are sedentary remain inactive even when they have free time. This made something very clear: Behavior is not shaped by available time, but by what has become the default habit.

⚡

Insight 2 — Motivation triggers action, but cannot sustain it

Many participants described starting with motivation—but not maintaining it. Motivation: appears inconsistently, fades quickly, is easily replaced by more immediate or enjoyable alternatives. This reframed my thinking: Motivation is not a system—it is a spark.

🌍

Insight 3 — Environment shapes behavior more than access or affordability

I initially assumed resources like gyms or equipment would matter more. But what actually mattered was: whether movement felt naturally supported in daily context, whether the environment made inactivity the easier option. This shifted my perspective: The key is not providing tools—but designing for context.

🧭

Insight 4 — People struggle not because they lack goals, but because they lack actionable clarity

Many users had the desire to be active. But they struggled at the moment of action because:

  • they didn't know what to do
  • they didn't know how to start
  • they couldn't connect intention to immediate behavior

This became a critical realization: Without clear "what" and "how," intention collapses.

At this point, I realized my initial assumptions—time, motivation, resources—were not wrong, but incomplete. They described symptoms, not structure. So I reframed the problem again.

Direction

Design should focus on making action immediate, contextual, and easier than inaction

The question evolved into:

How might we help people shift from sedentary behavior to active behavior without relying on motivation?

From the insights, I identified four key design directions:

🔔

1. From awareness to action: users need reminders that include "what to do next"

People are already aware of sedentary risks. What they lack is not awareness—but direction in the moment. So reminders must become: contextual, actionable, immediately usable. Not just "you've been sitting too long," but: "here's something simple you can do right now."

🤸

2. From rigid exercise to flexible movement choices

People are more likely to act when options: fit their current context, require minimal preparation, feel easy to start immediately. Diversity in movement lowers psychological resistance.

🪞

3. From task-based exercise to habit-based identity

This was a major shift in my thinking. Instead of designing for: "help users exercise more" I started designing for: "help users become someone who naturally stays active"

💛

4. From pressure to support: reducing emotional friction is essential

I noticed emotional barriers often come before physical ones: guilt from past failure, anxiety about long-term health, frustration from inconsistency. These emotions quietly reduce the likelihood of starting again. So the design must also: reduce emotional resistance, not just physical effort.

Strategy

StepUp turns movement into small, frequent, low-friction actions embedded in daily life

Based on these directions, I explored concepts centered around a mobile experience—because it is already deeply integrated into users' daily routines. Early concepts included:

📲smart notifications that respond to inactivity
🃏quick-switch activity cards for immediate decisions
👓AR-based movement guidance
🎙️real-time voice feedback during activities

But I stepped back and asked a more important question: What should the system fundamentally optimize for?

The answer became clear: frequency over intensity, immediacy over planning, integration over separation.

So StepUp is designed around a habit-building loop:

🔔Trigger: contextual reminders based on inactivity
🏃Action: short, effortless movement suggestions
🎉Reward: immediate feedback and satisfaction
🔁Reinforcement: repeated success builds identity

This led to a core shift in the product philosophy: The goal is no longer to complete workouts. The goal is to build identity: "I am someone who stays active."

From this strategy, features emerged directly:

🎯Personalized activity filters — based on physical discomfort or context
🗺️Contextual guidance — clear "what to do next" in the moment
☀️Routine-aware suggestions — embedded in daily behavior patterns
🌱Low-pressure progress system — remove guilt and over-complex goals
✨Simplified interaction — reduce decision fatigue, prioritize action

Core Design Philosophy

🚀Easy to start — Lower the barrier so users can begin without overthinking
⏱️Short duration — Keep activities brief enough to fit any moment
🔁High frequency — Encourage many small actions over one big effort
✨Reduce friction — Remove every unnecessary step between intention and action
☀️Embed into daily routines — Attach movement to habits users already have
🌱Reinforce identity through repetition — Build the feeling of being someone who moves

Closing Reflection

This project shifted from designing exercise tools to designing behavioral change systems

What changed most throughout this project was not the solution—but my understanding of the problem itself.

💭

I started with:

"How do we make people exercise more?"

→
💡

I ended with:

"How do we make movement the easiest and most natural choice in daily life?"
